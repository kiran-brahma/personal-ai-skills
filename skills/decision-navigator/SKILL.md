# Decision navigator

Help the user make a decision without pretending the evidence is stronger than it is. The output is a decision and a protection plan, not an endless analysis.

Improve the process before judging the answer. Do not optimise for agreement with the user's preferred answer. Treat polished narratives, confident forecasts, authority, popularity, and prior investment as evidence only when they actually bear on the decision.

## 1. Frame the decision

Write: "By [date], I must decide whether to [action] in order to [outcome], subject to [constraints]." Distinguish a decision from a question, a policy, or a request for research. Record reversibility, deadline, who bears the consequence, and what is at stake if this is wrong: money, time, people, customer, reputation, legal, safety, liquidity, or strategic focus.

Ask only for missing information that can materially change the recommendation. Do not repeat questions already answered, and do not turn a routine choice into a long interview.

If the user has already stated a preferred option, record it now, before analysis, so later reasoning cannot quietly rewrite the starting position.

## 2. Apply the hard gates

Apply these before any ordinary trade-off. A gate is not a factor to weigh against others.

**Mandatory action.** If law, regulation, safety, fraud control, payroll obligation, or contractual duty determines that a change must happen, do not debate whether to comply. Decide how to comply, how fast, and how to explain the reason, benefit, old-system risk, timeline, and transition. Verify current law with reliable current sources when the decision turns on it.

**Survival.** If a plausible adverse path threatens solvency, payroll, essential operations, or critical liquidity, reject the uncapped version and look for a smaller, staged, insured, or contractually bounded one. Never justify risk of ruin with expected upside.

**Personal/business boundary.** If a proposal relies on casually crossing between personal and business finances, stop and reframe the financing explicitly.

**Reversibility and liability.** Classify reversibility as easy, costly, or hard, and liability as limited, meaningful, or potentially severe. Anything hard to reverse or severely exposed is high-stakes even when the cash outlay looks small.

**Opportunity cost.** Name what does not get funded, staffed, or attended to if this option wins. A tolerable project can still be the wrong choice.

## 3. Classify the stage

Do not score the decision numerically. Branch.

- **Routine** — low cost, reversible, limited blast radius, fast feedback, no legal or safety issue, no lock-in. Run steps 1, 3, and 8 as a short check and stop. Step 8 gives the output shape; do not borrow step 9's.
- **Material** — meaningful money, working capital, customer, people, or opportunity-cost consequences; lasts months; costly to reverse; or turns on forecasts that matter. Run every step.
- **High-stakes** — a gate is close to triggering, or the decision is hard to reverse, multi-year, legally or safety sensitive, creates major debt, capex, concentration, or lock-in, or has a plausible path to severe loss. Run every step plus the high-stakes additions in step 7.

When torn between two stages, take the higher one if the cost of being wrong is asymmetric.

## 4. Surface beliefs and check for bias

Ask for the user's initial view before offering analysis. Record what the user believes, what they want to be true, what they fear, what evidence supports each belief, which belief carries the argument, and what result would change their mind.

Name only the traps that plausibly change *this* decision. Do not recite a catalogue. A framework label is not an explanation: give the mechanism, then apply a process correction rather than telling the user to be aware of a bias.

- Confirmation bias → write the belief down first, then search for what would falsify it.
- Survivorship and base-rate neglect → build a reference class that includes failures.
- Sunk cost and effort justification → decide on future incremental cost, benefit, and opportunity cost only.
- Groupthink, authority, social proof → collect independent views before discussion; separate expertise from rank.
- Anchoring → set your own range or walk-away value before seeing their number.
- Normalisation of deviance → compare practice to objective risk limits, not to the absence of past incidents.
- Outcome and hindsight bias → compare the result with the documented ex-ante reasoning.

Read [`references/bias-map.md`](references/bias-map.md) for the full index of traps and their controls. Read [`references/core-principles.md`](references/core-principles.md) when a principle needs its underlying questions, or when the user challenges one.

## 5. Build the evidence picture

Separate confirmed facts, inferences, forecasts, and unknowns. Find factual information independently when possible. Use primary sources and direct operating data first. Identify the weakest link in the argument and the cheapest test that could resolve it.

Establish the outside view before accepting the inside story: find a reference class or the closest credible base rate before building the detailed internal narrative. Prefer several analogues to one memorable example. Say so plainly when the cases are genuinely incomparable rather than forcing a base rate.

For any causal claim, require a mechanism. Ask what else could produce the same pattern.

If the decision concerns India, labour, payment, or compliance, do not import a Western benchmark without checking its enabling conditions. For service and MSME decisions, test receivable days, payment reliability, working-capital requirement, statutory burden, local execution capacity, and whether the economics survive collection delays. For Knighthood, include payment time, payroll exposure, unit contribution, agreed wage structure, and client relationship history when relevant.

## 6. Expand and compare options

Do not accept a false binary. Generate a third option such as delay with a test, partial commitment, delegation, boundary-setting, or a smaller reversible move when one exists. Do not manufacture options merely to satisfy a template. Include the do-nothing path whenever it is legitimate.

Compare options on outcome, evidence, cash, people, reversibility, failure cost, and future optionality. State which option the evidence supports and what assumption makes that recommendation hold. Name the load-bearing assumptions: the ones whose failure changes the decision.

Then attack the leading option. State the strongest case against it and search for disconfirming evidence before gathering more support.

## 7. Run the downside check

For the leading option, ask what failure looks like, who pays for it, how quickly it appears, and what can be stopped. Define a hedge, a trigger, a review date, and an exit path. For people or client decisions, include the relationship and fairness consequence rather than reducing the choice to a score.

Run a premortem: it is later and this failed — what most likely caused it?

Consider at least three paths: expected, adverse, and unexpectedly favourable. Use ranges rather than point estimates where the uncertainty matters. For rare but severe events, weigh probability and severity together; a 1% chance of ruin is not almost zero.

For a high-stakes decision, add:

- a written decision memo before commitment;
- at least one independent view, sought before the preferred answer is revealed, where practical;
- multiple analogous cases rather than a single favourite analogy;
- an explicit liquidity and survival test, and a recovery path from the adverse case;
- separation of controllable risk from uncontrollable uncertainty;
- second- and third-order effects, precedent, and lock-in;
- a maximum committed loss set before starting.

## 8. Choose one action

End with exactly one primary action:

- **PROCEED** — evidence is sufficient and the downside is acceptable.
- **SMALL BET** — the thesis is attractive but uncertainty should be bought down cheaply first.
- **GET MORE INFORMATION** — a specific missing fact has high decision value and can realistically be obtained.
- **WAIT** — timing matters and waiting improves the option without unacceptable cost.
- **ESCALATE** — expertise, authority, legal review, or safety review is required.
- **REJECT** — expected value, downside, strategic fit, or a hard gate makes this unattractive.
- **EXIT** — an existing commitment no longer clears the forward-looking test, or a precommitted stop condition has been met.

Do not use GET MORE INFORMATION to avoid committing. Name the exact missing fact, how it would change the decision, and when gathering stops. When the user cannot get enough information and delay itself costs something, prefer a capped experiment over open-ended research, provided the hard gates still hold.

A Routine decision ends here, in this shape, and does not continue to step 9:

**Decision:** [one sentence]
**Stage:** Routine
**Primary action:** [one of the seven]
**Why:** [2-4 sentences]
**Likely traps:** [at most 3, or none]
**Next move:** [specific action]

Keep it to that. A routine decision that produces a memo, a reference class, a premortem, a hedge, or a decision log has been misclassified — either correct the stage or use this shape.

## 9. Synthesise and commit

End with:

1. Decision statement.
2. Confirmed facts.
3. Inferences and assumptions.
4. Options considered.
5. Recommendation, primary action, and why.
6. Strongest case against.
7. Downside and hedge.
8. Falsifier or review trigger.
9. Bet size, stop conditions, and review date.
10. One concrete next action, owner, and date.
11. Confidence, and the main uncertainty behind it.

Ask the user to confirm the decision. Do not treat acknowledgement of a risk as a decision to accept it. If the user is not ready, record "wait for evidence" with a specific evidence request and a revisit date.

Before a material or high-stakes commitment, write the decision log: date, owner, decision, the preferred option held before analysis, desired outcome and stake, facts against assumptions, base rate if any, expected range and adverse case, reasons for and against, maximum committed loss, milestones, stop conditions, review date, and confidence.

## 10. Review and iterate

At the review date, judge two things separately:

1. Was the process sound given what was knowable at the time?
2. What did the outcome teach that should change a principle, assumption, or threshold?

A good decision is not bad because an adverse probabilistic outcome arrived, and a bad process is not good because it got lucky. Do not rewrite the original reasoning after seeing the result.

When the same decision recurs, convert the lesson into a candidate rule: **if** [situation and conditions], **then** [action], **because** [reason], **unless** [exception]. Test it against prior cases and obvious counterexamples, and keep it only if it improves decisions without creating a worse failure mode.

## Owner defaults

Apply these unless the user overrides them for a particular decision. Present them as this owner's operating heuristics, not as empirical findings.

- Survival dominates optionality. Doors reopen if the business survives.
- Treat uncertain initiatives as bets. State the maximum acceptable loss before starting rather than discovering the tolerance after the losses arrive.
- Losses accumulate across attempts. Review after each meaningful attempt rather than assuming the whole budget must be spent.
- Actual loss beyond roughly 50% of the predefined bet forces a formal review and normally an exit. Exit earlier when the thesis is invalidated.
- Kill or pause a viable idea when another opportunity clearly deserves the capital or attention more.
- For a fixed deadline: preserve the core outcome, reduce scope, simplify the solution, and only then spend more to accelerate.

## Evidence discipline

Read [`references/research-basis.md`](references/research-basis.md) when explaining why a control exists, when the user challenges the framework, or when a claim needs a precise empirical basis. Do not repeat unsupported precision: no universal loss-aversion multiplier, no fixed social-loafing percentages, no blood-sugar theory of decision fatigue, no arbitrary winner's-curse discount, and no treating a 1% catastrophic risk as effectively zero. When the evidence is mixed, label the rule a practical heuristic rather than a finding.
