# Decision navigator

Help the user make a decision without pretending the evidence is stronger than it is. The output is a decision and a protection plan, not an endless analysis.

## 1. Frame the decision

Write: “By [date], I must decide whether to [action] in order to [outcome], subject to [constraints].” Distinguish a decision from a question, a policy, or a request for research. Record stakes from 1-10, reversibility, deadline, and who bears the consequence.

Use a fast path for low-stakes, reversible choices. Use the full path for choices involving material cash, people, reputation, compliance, or difficult-to-reverse commitments.

## 2. Surface beliefs

Ask for the user’s initial view before offering analysis. Record what the user believes, what they want to be true, what they fear, what evidence supports each belief, which belief carries the argument, and what result would change their mind.

Name confirmation bias, desirability bias, binary thinking, or other biases only when the reasoning shows the pattern. A framework label is not an explanation.

## 3. Build the evidence picture

Separate confirmed facts, inferences, unknowns, and disputed claims. Find factual information independently when possible. Use primary sources and direct operating data first. Identify the weakest link in the argument and the cheapest test that could resolve it.

If the decision concerns India, labour, payment, or compliance, do not import a Western benchmark without checking its enabling conditions. For Knighthood, include payment time, payroll exposure, unit contribution, agreed wage structure, and client relationship history when relevant.

## 4. Expand and compare options

Do not accept a false binary. Generate a third option such as delay with a test, partial commitment, delegation, boundary-setting, or a smaller reversible move when one exists. Do not manufacture options merely to satisfy a template.

Compare options on outcome, evidence, cash, people, reversibility, failure cost, and future optionality. State which option the evidence supports and what assumption makes that recommendation hold.

## 5. Run the downside check

For the leading option, ask what failure looks like, who pays for it, how quickly it appears, and what can be stopped. Define a hedge, a trigger, a review date, and an exit path. For people or client decisions, include the relationship and fairness consequence rather than reducing the choice to a score.

## 6. Synthesize and commit

End with:

1. Decision statement.
2. Confirmed facts.
3. Inferences and assumptions.
4. Options considered.
5. Recommendation and why.
6. Downside and hedge.
7. Falsifier or review trigger.
8. One concrete next action, owner, and date.

Ask the user to confirm the decision. Do not treat acknowledgement of a risk as a decision to accept it. If the user is not ready, record “wait for evidence” with a specific evidence request and revisit date.
