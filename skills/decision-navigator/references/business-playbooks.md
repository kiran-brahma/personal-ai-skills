## 1. Customer / contract acceptance

Test:
- contribution margin after realistic cost-to-serve
- payment terms vs actual expected collection behavior
- receivable days and maximum working-capital exposure
- customer concentration created by the deal
- service capacity and operational distraction
- penalty/liability clauses and asymmetry of obligations
- statutory/compliance burden by location
- scope creep and exception load
- price escalation / wage inflation pass-through
- termination rights and exit cost
- reputational spillover if service fails

Bias traps: revenue-size halo, scarcity, social proof, sunk cost from sales effort, liking, anchor on headline contract value, overconfidence in collections.

Prefer a smaller pilot/site/region if service quality or collection behavior is uncertain and the contract allows it.

## 2. Pricing and credit terms

Before accepting the customer's anchor, estimate:
- floor price based on cost, risk, and required return
- financing cost of receivables
- probability and timing of collection
- cost of exceptions / customization
- volume sensitivity
- precedent for future renewals/customers
- cost to exit or replace the customer

Do not treat revenue growth as value creation when additional working capital or credit risk absorbs the return.

## 3. Hiring, firing, promotion

Define the role outcome before judging the person.

Check:
- evidence from comparable work, not charisma or pedigree alone
- whether past performance was selection effect or actual contribution
- reference checks and work samples where appropriate
- role clarity, authority, resources, and incentives
- external/system causes before attributing failure only to the individual
- reversibility: probation, staged responsibility, trial project
- cost of a false positive vs false negative
- effect on team norms and precedent

For firing, separate sunk cost/training effort from expected future contribution. For promotion, distinguish performance in the current role from evidence for the next role.

## 4. Vendor selection

Test:
- problem solved and measurable outcome
- total cost of ownership, not quoted price only
- switching and data-export cost
- implementation burden on the team
- vendor concentration and dependency
- security/privacy/compliance where relevant
- service levels and enforcement
- proof from comparable customers
- reference incentives: are references selected only from successes?
- exit plan and data/process continuity

Prefer a pilot when integration or adoption risk is material.

## 5. Capex and software

Ask:
- what operational problem exists today?
- what happens if nothing is bought?
- utilization needed to justify the purchase
- implementation and training cost
- internal process/data prerequisites
- maintenance, renewal, support, and migration cost
- lock-in and reversibility
- whether a simpler/manual version can prove the workflow first
- measurable success milestone

Bias traps: technology halo, vendor anchor, effort justification, information bias, action bias, sunk cost after implementation starts.

## 6. New product / business launch

Require:
- explicit customer/problem hypothesis
- reference class of similar launches, including failures
- smallest test that can falsify the core thesis
- maximum financial and attention bet
- leading indicators before full-scale economics arrive
- distribution/sales assumptions
- operational and compliance dependencies
- opportunity cost vs the current core business
- kill criteria and review date

Do not demand certainty. Buy information with small, reversible experiments while protecting survival.

## 7. Geographic expansion

India-specific checks when relevant:
- state/local statutory requirements
- availability and quality of local hiring/supervision
- customer concentration and pipeline quality
- wage, travel, lodging, and supervision economics
- receivable behavior and working-capital requirement
- local vendor/partner reliability
- serviceability and escalation time
- central-team distraction

Do not assume success in one city/state transfers unchanged to another.

## 8. Process and system changes

First classify the change:

**Mandatory:** law, safety, fraud, payroll, contractual, or compliance requirement. Optimize implementation, not whether to comply.

**Discretionary:** compare current-state pain with implementation cost and expected improvement.

For either:
- map current reality before designing the future state
- identify the typical flow first, then important exceptions
- separate frequent exceptions from rare edge cases
- define transition ownership and rollback where possible
- communicate reason, benefit, old-system risk, timeline, and how-to
- measure whether the new system is actually better

Do not normalize unsafe deviations merely because prior incidents did not occur.

## 9. Incentives and KPIs

For every metric ask:
- what behavior does it reward?
- how can it be gamed?
- what important outcome can be harmed while the metric improves?
- who controls the metric and who bears the external cost?
- does the measure create short-term gains at long-term expense?
- is individual ownership visible where group effort can diffuse accountability?
- could financial rewards crowd out duty, identity, mastery, or customer orientation?

Use a small set of metrics with counter-metrics for major failure modes when needed.

## 10. Continue, pause, or kill a project

Ignore past spend except where it changes future options.

Ask:
- if this project did not already exist, would we start it today on the remaining terms?
- what has changed in the thesis or odds?
- what future cash/time is required?
- what is the best alternative use of those resources?
- are milestones being missed repeatedly?
- has actual loss reached a precommitted review/exit threshold?
- is continuation driven by ego, consistency, effort, or fear of admitting error?

A viable project can still be killed for opportunity cost.

## 11. Cash, debt, and borrowing

Test:
- source and reliability of repayment
- cash-flow timing, not accounting profit alone
- downside if customer collections slip
- covenant/security implications
- refinancing dependence
- variable-rate or currency exposure where relevant
- whether borrowing funds a temporary mismatch or structurally bad economics
- survival under an adverse but plausible case

Do not use personal funds as an informal default solution to a business financing problem.

## 12. Compliance and safety

Hard rule: if the requirement is mandatory, do not trade compliance against convenience as though both are ordinary options.

Check:
- what the current rule actually requires
- effective date and jurisdiction
- safety/legal severity
- current deviation and exposure
- transition steps and owner
- proof/audit trail
- exception handling

Normalization-of-deviance test: "Would we still call this acceptable if an incident occurred tomorrow?"

Use current authoritative sources when law/regulation is part of the decision.

## 13. Crisis decisions

Sequence:
1. stabilize immediate safety/liquidity/customer harm
2. identify what is known vs assumed
3. preserve reversible options
4. avoid irreversible action merely to appear decisive
5. define next information checkpoint
6. assign clear owner
7. review after the situation stabilizes

Do not confuse activity with control.

## 14. Major exceptions

Before granting an exception ask:
- why is the rule being bypassed?
- is this a one-off or a precedent?
- what is the maximum blast radius?
- what compensating control is needed?
- when does the exception expire?
- who owns the risk?
- how will it be logged and reviewed?

Repeated exceptions are evidence that either the rule or the operating reality needs redesign.

## 15. Fixed-deadline projects

When the date has a real external rationale:
1. identify the non-negotiable outcome
2. reduce scope while preserving that outcome
3. simplify design/complexity
4. parallelize or add resources where useful
5. spend more only when the value of meeting the deadline justifies it
6. document what moves to a later phase

Do not protect an ideal scope at the cost of missing the actual objective.
