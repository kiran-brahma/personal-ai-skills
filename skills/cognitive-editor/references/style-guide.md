# Style Guide — Reference

Two layers. **UNIVERSAL** applies to any prose, any author, any subject. **KB VOICE**
applies only in KB-ECOSYSTEM MODE (Kiran's own blog/build-log writing).

---

## UNIVERSAL

### George Orwell's Six Rules

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut out a word, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon word if an everyday word exists.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

---

### Word Choice

Self-check before keeping a word: is it short? Would you say it to a friend? Does nearly
everyone know it? Would a good writer from a century ago have used it? If not, look for the
plainer word.

**Prefer plain over dressed-up:**
buy not purchase · let not permit · help not aid · show not demonstrate · get not obtain ·
make not manufacture · set up not establish · give up not relinquish · break not violate ·
hand out not distribute · workers not workforce · pay not compensation · sales not revenue ·
rich people not high-net-worth individuals · goods/stock not inventory · NGOs not civil
society · fighting/war not conflict · spies not the intelligence community · scholars not
the academic community · refuses to not demonstrates an unwillingness to · people sleeping
rough not homelessness · torture, murder, massacres not human-rights abuses · dead civilians
not civilian casualties or collateral damage · death not mortality · vote-rigging not voting
irregularities · lay-offs not redundancies · poor people not the underprivileged · rape,
groping not sexual assault (be specific, not euphemistic) · battle not kinetic action.

**Avoid pomposity:** meetings dressed up as summits, detail dressed up as granular,
brainstorming dressed up as ideation, lessons dressed up as learnings, appearances dressed up
as optics. None of these say more than the plain word — only louder.

**Avoid novel verbing:** to action, to gift, to interface, to whiteboard, to leverage, to
showcase, to source, to segue, to target. A few verbed nouns have earned their place through
long use (to host, to contact). Most new ones just annoy.

**Avoid misdirection dressed as neutrality:** "synergy" after a merger usually means fewer
workers. Treat euphemism as a flag, not a style choice — ask what it's hiding.

---

### Banned words and phrases

Corporate jargon and vague intensifiers — flag on sight:
align, crushing it, deep dive, delve, democratize, disruptive, drive/driving, ecosystem,
elevate, empower, flywheel, foster, game-changer, garner, guru, growth-hack, harness, human
capital, hustle (grind sense), innovative, journey, landscape, leverage (non-financial),
ninja, north star, optimize, paradigm, realm, reimagine, robust, rockstar, scalable,
seamless, solutions, streamline, synergy, tapestry, testament, 10x, unlock, unleash,
unparalleled, world-class, aspirational, facilitate, high-profile, iconic, key (as
adjective), major, narrative, participate in, passionate, proactive, players (outside
sports), prestigious, reputational, savvy, stakeholders, supportive, surreal, trajectory,
transformative, vision, benchmarks, stimulus, yield (as buzzword), bubble, optics.

Hedge words that weaken a claim you've already earned — cut them once you're sure: rather,
somewhat, possibly, mostly, actually, really.

Redundant or euphemistic pairs — say the plain one: compensation → pay, impairments →
losses, letting people go → firing, sub-investment grade → junk bonds, headed up by →
headed by, bought up → bought, sold off → sold, freed up → freed.

Watch for words that mean the same thing every time they're used, even when the underlying
event differs: crisis, scandal, historic, rhetoric. Reserve them for when they're true, or
they lose their force for the one time you need them.

### Clichés and dead metaphors — replace

game-changer · going forward (meaning "in future") · the green light · grinding to a halt ·
heavy lifting · honeymoon period · level playing-field · paradigm shift · perfect storm ·
poster child · rack up · ramping up · tipping point · too close to call · wake-up call ·
low-hanging fruit · elephant in the room · circle back · let's take this offline · quantum
leap · inflection point · exponential growth (when the growth isn't actually exponential).

**Avoid extended or mixed business metaphors** built on top of these — the kind that
"chunk up to a meta-level" or promise to "water strategic shoots." If a metaphor needs a
second metaphor to explain it, cut both and say the thing plainly.

---

### Concrete over abstract

Concrete nouns are ones a reader can picture. Abstract nouns describe concepts you'll never
stub your toe on. Some abstractions are fine (love, time) — but nominalisations, the boring
nouns built from verbs ("observation" from "observe"), are usually a sign the sentence has
lost its actor. Put the person back in: not "a decision was made to cut costs" but "I cut
costs." Not "the rise in salaries" but "pay is growing" — and if you can name who, name them.

### Grammar and Sentence Craft

**Length:** Paragraphs max 3 sentences, one subject, sequential. Sentences max 20 words,
target 8–20. Short sentences aren't a style tic — working memory is limited, and every full
stop gives the reader's mind a chance to reset before the next idea lands.

**Active over passive, with three exceptions:**
1. To preserve focus on the subject already established, rather than introducing a new one.
2. To preserve old-then-new information flow, when the previous sentence's subject should
   stay the subject of this one.
3. When the agent is genuinely unknown or unimportant.

Outside those three, passive voice is evasion — it lets a writer skip who did what to whom,
and readers notice the gap even when they can't name it.

**One-read test:** if a sentence has to be read twice to parse, it has failed. Reword rather
than leave the reader to guess between two possible meanings.

**Punctuation:**
- Full stop: your most frequent tool. Signals the reader can clear their mind and restart.
- Semicolon: connects two independent clauses without "and" or "but." Don't overuse.
- Colon: promises something, then delivers it. More directional than a link.
- Comma: a breath-pause. Never use it to splice two independent sentences together.
- Dash: dramatic, and therefore easy to overuse — cap it at one per paragraph, and prefer a
  comma, colon, or new sentence where you can.
- Hyphen: groups words that should read as one unit.

**Correct usage:**
- Who/whom: rephrase as a statement — "you saw whom" if "him" fits, "who" if "he" fits.
- After a preposition, use the object pronoun: "between you and me," not "between you and I."
- Singular subjects take a singular verb; watch -ics words (economics, politics) which are
  singular alone but often plural preceded by "the."
- Parallel structure: items in a list, and both/and or either/or pairs, must match in form.
- Dangling modifiers invite absurd readings — readers attach the modifier to the first noun
  they meet, so if that's the wrong one, recast the sentence.
- Singular "they" is natural after someone, anyone, no one, everyone — use it to avoid
  gendered pronouns rather than defaulting to "he."
- "That" for restrictive clauses (no comma), "which" for non-restrictive (set off by commas).
- In reported past speech, use "might," not "may": "he said they might win," not "may win."
  Use "may" only when real, present-tense possibility or permission is meant.

### Numbers

- Never start a sentence with a figure — spell the number out instead.
- Figures for 11 upward; words below that. "20th century," "a 29-year-old man," "40-fold."
- Use the % sign, not "per cent." "Percentage," never "%age."
- Dates: month, day, year. Say "between June 10th and 14th," not "June 10th–14th." Give the
  actual date rather than "last week" or "next month."
- Prefer round fractions to percentages when they're clearer: "a third," not "36%."
- Big numbers need context: rate per person, share of GDP, comparison to a known quantity.
  "Health care is 18% of GDP, $3.6trn a year" beats "a whopping $3.6trn."
- Compare like with like — same adjustment basis, same time period, same currency basis.
- Keep significant figures consistent; two is usually enough for a general reader.
- A 400% increase means multiplying by five, not four — a 100% increase means doubling.
- Percentage change and percentage-point change are different things. If growth goes from 7%
  to 5%, that's a fall of two percentage points, not "a fall of 2%."
- Don't drop a trailing zero that matters: 8% and 8.0% imply different ranges of precision.
- For rates (speed, height, weight), say "half as heavy," not "twice as light" — division
  applies to the measured quantity, not its inverse framing.

### Statistical and causal literacy

- Correlation is not causation. Check for reverse causation and third factors before
  asserting a causal link. Be doubly sceptical of a study from an interested party that
  skipped independent review.
- Statistical significance is not the same as effect size. State both if you're stating
  either — a real effect can still be tiny, and a big-sounding effect can be statistical
  noise.
- Specify mean, median, or mode when you say "average." Median is more honest than mean when
  a few extreme values would otherwise distort the picture.
- A single study is weaker evidence than a meta-study that combines many. Watch for
  "p-hacking" — rerunning a study design until the desired threshold is hit by chance.
- Mind baselines. Doubling a risk that starts out tiny is still tiny in absolute terms —
  state the absolute number alongside the relative change.

### Business and finance terms — use precisely

- Gross profit: revenue minus direct cost of goods sold. Operating profit: gross profit
  minus wider running costs (rent, shipping, depreciation, overheads) — usually the more
  useful number.
- "Adjusted EBITDA" is often a customised figure built to flatter results — flag it as such.
- Net income is the true bottom line but includes one-off items (lawsuit payouts, asset
  sales) that can distort the year-on-year picture.
- Cashflow tracks money actually moving; it isn't the same as sales. Free cashflow is what's
  left after capital investment — a closer read on what a company can actually spend.
- A balance sheet is a snapshot at one point in time, not a flow.
- For banks: a loan is an asset (future payments owed to the bank); a deposit is a liability
  (owed back to the depositor). Capital usually means shareholders' equity; reserves usually
  means cash, including central-bank reserves.
- Always use real (inflation-adjusted) GDP for growth comparisons. If you use nominal GDP,
  say so explicitly.
- Specify which debt you mean — household, corporate, public, or total — and note that the
  same ratio means different things for a poor country versus a rich one.
- Spell out jargon for a lay reader in a phrase or two; don't assume familiarity with terms
  that are second nature to insiders.

### Editing practice

- Be specific: not "I didn't like this part" but what failed and why.
- Keep it about the text, not the person — repeated "you" reads as accusatory.
- If something's clearly wrong but the fix isn't obvious, ask the author what they meant.
  The mismatch between intention and result usually surfaces on its own once asked.
- Batch all queries into one pass rather than sending them one at a time.
- Praise specifically. "Nice job" teaches nothing; naming exactly what worked does.

---

### Drafting discipline

- Get a first draft down fast, then edit it hard. Attachment to a draft because of the effort
  it took is the sunk-cost fallacy — don't let it protect weak material.
- Reverse-outline: pull the one main point out of each paragraph and check the sequence
  actually holds together.
- Read the draft aloud. It slows you down and exposes what a silent read skips over.
- "Simplify, then exaggerate": be clear about real caveats and uncertainty, but once you've
  earned a claim, state it — cut "arguably," "some say," "it might be the case that" where
  the evidence already supports something stronger.
- Steelman the opposing case — cite its strongest form, not its weakest. Avoid false
  balance: weigh the evidence and let the argument fall where it lands, rather than
  presenting two sides as equal by default.

---

### Tightening

- Cut "there is / there are" openers: "there was a 23% increase in sales" → "sales increased
  by 23%."
- Turn negative constructions positive where it reads cleaner: "it is not easy to tell" → "it
  is hard to tell."
- Watch for double- or triple-negative sentences that don't actually parse on a close read.
- Cut adjectives and adverbs that aren't earning their place — one strong verb often beats a
  weak verb plus an adverb ("sprint" beats "run swiftly").
- Cut wordy phrases: absolute certainty → certainty · track record → record · end result →
  result · final outcome → outcome · the fact that → that (mostly) · role model → model ·
  past/lived/personal experience → experience · safe haven → haven · located in → in · in
  close proximity to → close to · large-scale → big · weather conditions → weather.

### Correct word usage (common errors)

Alternate = every other. Alternative = one of two. · Among = three or more. Between = two
only. · Continuous = uninterrupted. · Convince → persuade (you persuade someone to act; you
convince them of a fact). · Critique = noun. Criticise = verb. · Disinterested = impartial.
Uninterested = bored. · Effective = with effect. Cost-effective = good value — if it's cheap,
say cheap. · Enormity = a crime, not immensity. · Practicable = feasible. Practical = useful.
· Presently = soon, not "at present." · Systemic = relating to a whole system. Systematic =
methodical. · "Which" adds information; "that" defines it. · Data and media are plural. ·
Strategy/strategic: avoid outside a genuine military or competitive-positioning sense. ·
Issues: be precise — "concern" or "number," as the case requires. · Environment: avoid where
a plainer word will do. · Challenge: usually just drop it and name the actual problem. ·
Crescendo means growing louder, not the peak. · Epicentre is the point on the surface above
an earthquake, not its "heart." · The eye of a storm is calm, not the most intense part. ·
Calculus deals with limits and rates of change, not "calculation" in general.

---

### Readability target

Flesch Reading Ease 60–70. Short sentences. Active voice. Concrete words. This is the
default target for any prose passing through this audit, regardless of author.

## KB VOICE (KB-ECOSYSTEM MODE ONLY)

- Currency: ₹, not "Rs." Dollar conversion in brackets if it helps a non-Indian reader.
- Indian numerals: Lakhs, Crores. Add millions/billions in brackets for a global reader.
- Person: "I," not "we," unless the action was genuinely collective — personal accountability
  requires the first person.
- Contractions are fine; don't overdo them.
- Abbreviations: spell out in full on first use, then use the short form.
