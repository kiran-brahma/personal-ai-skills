# Customer value proposition

Move from an idea or offer to a promise that a specific customer can recognise and test. Do not begin with a slogan. Begin with the customer’s behaviour and the transaction.

## Rules

- Keep user, buyer, payer, decision-maker, and beneficiary distinct.
- Ask one question at a time. Skip questions already answered with specific evidence.
- Prefer observed behaviour, current spending, workarounds, complaints, and switching behaviour over stated preference.
- Treat 4U, 3D, Lean Canvas, TAM/SAM/SOM, or similar frameworks as prompts. They do not prove demand.
- Replace “10x,” “disruptive,” and “seamless” with a measurable difference or remove the claim.
- Make India, industry, cash, compliance, and staffing constraints explicit when they affect the promise.

## Adaptive workflow

Use only the phases needed for the current question. A new offer usually needs all of them; a refinement may need only phases 2-4.

### 1. Idea and customer

Capture the idea in 30 words or fewer. Classify it as a feature, product, service, or business hypothesis. Name the first customer segment narrowly enough to contact one person this week.

### 2. Problem and job

Write the problem as:

> When [specific situation], [specific customer] struggles to [job], causing [observable consequence].

Ask what the customer is trying to accomplish, what happens today, and what the problem costs. Use five whys only when each answer remains grounded in a real example. Reframe the problem at least twice when the initial framing may be solution-led.

### 3. Alternative and promise

Map the status quo, direct competitors, indirect substitutes, and deliberate inaction. Record what each alternative does well and where it fails. Then draft:

> For [specific customer] who [observable problem], [offer] helps achieve [specific outcome] by [credible mechanism], unlike [main alternative].

The mechanism must be something the business can actually deliver. The outcome must belong to the customer, not the operator’s internal activity.

### 4. Value and adoption

Identify the gain, pain removed, switching cost, trust required, and first-use friction. Ask what would make the customer try the offer, continue using it, pay, refer it, or return to the old alternative. Include the cost of onboarding, training, integration, and failure.

### 5. Validation test

Choose the smallest test that can change the belief. Examples include observing the current workflow, asking for a paid pilot, replacing a manual step for one customer, or measuring repeat use after a real service delivery. Define the hypothesis, test, metric, time window, and falsifier.

## Deliverable

End with the target customer and buying situation, confirmed problem and evidence, current alternative, proposed promise and delivery mechanism, main adoption friction, assumptions and falsifiers, next validation test, and a confidence statement that reflects the weakest evidence link.

Do not advance to MVP features, pricing, or go-to-market until the user approves the problem and promise. If implementation follows, hand off to the relevant coding workflow rather than prescribing technical design here.
