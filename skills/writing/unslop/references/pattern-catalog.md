# Unslop pattern catalog

Use these as strong heuristics, not mechanical bans. If applying a rule makes the sentence less accurate, less natural, or harder to read, fix it another way or leave it alone.

## Content patterns

1. Cut puffery such as `pivotal moment`, `testament to`, `evolving landscape`, `setting the stage`, `deeply rooted`, and similar inflated framing. State what happened.
2. Avoid decorative name-dropping. Name a source only when its contribution matters.
3. Remove superficial `-ing` tails such as `highlighting`, `showcasing`, `fostering`, `reflecting`, or `ensuring` when they merely restate the sentence.
4. Remove promotional adjectives unless the task is promotional.
5. Replace vague attribution such as `experts believe` or `reports suggest` with the named source, or remove it when unsupported.
6. Replace formulaic `despite challenges...` constructions with the specific constraint and result.

## Language patterns

7. Prefer plain words over stock AI vocabulary such as `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `garner`, `interplay`, `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `underscore`, `tapestry`, or abstract `landscape`.
8. Prefer `is` and `has` over inflated substitutes such as `serves as`, `stands as`, `boasts`, or `features` when no distinction is gained.
9. Replace `not just X, but Y` with the actual point unless the contrast matters.
10. Do not force ideas into threes. Use the natural number.
11. Do not cycle synonyms for the same thing. Use one stable term.
12. Avoid false `from X to Y` ranges when X and Y are merely different topics.

## Style patterns

13. Avoid em dashes as a default. Prefer a period or comma. Use another construction when it reads better. Do not substitute awkward parentheses merely to obey the rule.
14. Use colons mainly before real lists, examples, definitions, or structures that benefit from them. Do not use them as habitual sentence connectors.
15. Do not bold every proper noun, acronym, or lead-in.
16. Avoid repetitive inline-header lists where a bold label merely repeats the sentence. Keep useful lead-ins when they add navigation.
17. Use sentence-case headings by default.
18. Remove decorative emojis from substantive prose unless the document's intended style calls for them.
19. Preserve the quotation-mark style required by the document. Do not churn punctuation without a readability reason.

## Communication artifacts

20. Remove stock chatbot phrases such as `I hope this helps`, `Of course`, `Certainly`, and generic offers to continue unless they serve a real conversational purpose.
21. Replace cutoff-style disclaimers with a specific limitation or omit them.
22. Remove sycophantic praise and automatic agreement.

## Filler and confidence

23. Compress filler: `in order to` becomes `to`; `due to the fact that` becomes `because`; delete `it is important to note that` when nothing is lost.
24. Reduce stacked hedging. Preserve only the uncertainty the evidence requires.
25. Replace generic conclusions with the specific fact, implication, next step, or unresolved point.

## Jargon and abstraction

26. Replace abstract metaphor nouns when a concrete word works: `substrate`, `wedge`, `vector`, `locus`, `nexus`, `primitive` as a noun, metaphorical `harness`, abstract `surface`, metaphorical `bedrock` or `scaffolding`, `modality`, `paradigm`, `gold-plating`, metaphorical `ratchet`, `evacuate`, `endgame`, `north star`, or `flywheel`.

Do not replace a term when it is a real technical term, a defined concept, a quotation, or the source's meaningful wording.

## Plain speech and working memory

27. Say what something does. Prefer mechanisms, facts, numbers, or consequences over vague sensory or emotional descriptions when the latter carry no information.
28. Split dense sentences when the reader must backtrack or hold several independent clauses in working memory. Around 20 to 30 words is often comfortable, but clarity decides.
29. Prefer active voice when the actor matters. Name the actor instead of hiding it behind `is/was + past participle`.
30. Cut adverbs that prop up weak verbs. Use a stronger verb, a measured fact, or no modifier.
31. Prefer the everyday word: `use` over `utilize` or metaphorical `leverage`, `help` over `facilitate`, `many` over `numerous`, `if` over `in the event that`.
32. Make every pronoun point to one obvious noun. Repeat the noun if needed.
33. Keep `only`, `not`, and similar modifiers next to the word they change.
34. Break long noun strings into clauses the reader can parse once.
35. Keep one name for one thing across the document.
36. Preserve small words such as articles and conjunctions when they remove ambiguity.

## Rhythm and voice

37. Vary sentence lengths. Short sentences create a clean break. Longer sentences can carry one idea with its condition or consequence.
38. Do not make every paragraph the same length or structure merely for visual symmetry.
39. Allow opinion only where the document mode permits it. Reference stays dry. Explanation, analysis, essays, and memos may carry a view.
40. Use first person only when it fits the intended speaker or document.
41. Specificity beats sterile polish. Replace generic concern or praise with the concrete reason when the evidence supports it.
42. Leave natural irregularity in the prose. Do not deliberately introduce errors, fragments, or slang to simulate humanity.

## Audit questions

Before returning the text, ask privately:

- Can any word disappear without losing meaning, qualification, tone, or accuracy?
- Does any sentence make the reader hold too much before reaching a full stop?
- Can every pronoun and modifier be resolved on the first read?
- Does each thing keep one stable name?
- Does each heading tell the reader what comes next?
- Did any edit strengthen, soften, generalise, or reinterpret the source?
- Did I add personality where the document should remain dry?
- Did I edit good prose only because I felt obliged to change something?
- What still makes this feel predictably AI-written?
