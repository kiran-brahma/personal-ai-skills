# Close-reading question bank

Ask in this order. `[lite]` marks the questions lite mode uses; complete mode asks every question in the selected sections. Ask one question per turn, in plain words; the label is for the record.

## 1. First take

1. **Story engagement** `[lite]`: Did you enjoy reading it? Did you want to keep reading to find out what happens next, or did you drift and have to force yourself to finish? Where did that happen?
2. **Key appeal**: What specific aspects appealed to you: tone, voice, character development, the central conflict or tension?
3. **Sharing the story** `[lite]`: Did you want to tell others about it? In one sentence, what would you tell them?
4. **Inspiration and curiosity**: Did you want to learn more, or wish you had thought of the story yourself? What might have led you towards such a topic?
5. **Topic novelty**: Is the topic new or controversial? Has it had significant media attention?
6. **Personal relevance**: Does the topic interest you, and who is affected by the issue overall?
7. **Broader ramifications**: What are the political, economic, or social ramifications of the topic?
8. **Public discourse contribution**: How should this story change how we think about the subject, and what does it add to public discussion or existing literature?

## 2. Story focus

1. **Central question** `[lite]`: What is the story's central question or argument? Boil it down to one sentence, then to six words.
2. **Headline essence**: Distil the story into a compelling headline that tells readers why to read it.
3. **Supporting evidence**: Does the most compelling information support the central theme? How?
4. **Distractions and flow**: Does anything distract from the main focus or cause a logical disconnect?
5. **Articulating the main idea**: Where is the main idea first stated, and how much detail is given there?
6. **Secondary themes**: Are there secondary themes, and how do they connect to the main one?
7. **Topic vs story** `[lite]`: A topic is not a story. What specific elements make this piece a story?

## 3. Information

1. **Essential data**: What are the most essential pieces of information, and does all of it belong?
2. **Omitted details**: What did the writer leave out? Was it deliberate, to let readers draw their own conclusions?
3. **Unanswered questions**: Did the story leave your questions unanswered, or leave you wanting more?
4. **Standout elements** `[lite]`: Which facts, numbers, quotes, anecdotes, characters, or details stand out most, and why?
5. **Reporting depth**: How does the writer show deep reporting throughout?
6. **Figurative devices**: How are similes, metaphors, analogies, or synecdoche used to explain concepts or stand for larger ideas?
7. **Perspectives and objectivity**: Does the story include conflicting points of view? Is the writer's own opinion evident or subtle?
8. **Impact and limitations**: If it focuses on a solution, does it show both real-world impact and clear limitations?
9. **Speculation**: Is there speculation, and how does the writer signal and justify it?

## 4. Structure

1. **Opening lede** `[lite]`: What makes the lede work (or not)? Why did the writer choose that episode to start?
2. **Organisational flow**: Is the organisation thematic or chronological? How does the narrative move back and forth in time?
3. **Nut graf** `[lite]`: Is there a nut graf? Where, how long, and how does it signpost the main themes?
4. **Transitions**: What kinds of transitions carry the reader between ideas? What makes them seamless?
5. **Narrative arc and flashbacks**: Do central characters, acts, or flashbacks recur to shape the structure?
6. **The kicker** `[lite]`: What kind of ending is it (quote, summary, call to the future, other)? Does it give closure?

## 5. Narrative

1. **Core narrative elements**: Which narrative elements are present: scenes, dialogue, character development, sensory detail?
2. **Central conflict** `[lite]`: What is the central conflict or source of tension, and how is it resolved by the end?
3. **Character and suspense**: How does the writer bring characters to life, build suspense, or use quotes to show inner life?
4. **Cinematic pacing**: Where does the writing zoom in on a scene, pull back, or foreshadow?
5. **Multiple threads and exposition**: How are narrative threads and technical or historical background woven together?
6. **Authorial presence**: Is the writer part of the narrative? Does that work?
7. **Scene crafting**: Which senses and physical details are highlighted, or left out, in the strongest scenes?

## 6. Language

1. **Diction and energy** `[lite]`: Which vivid nouns, active verbs, and formal or informal word choices give the story energy?
2. **Tone and accessibility**: What tone is set (witty, sombre, chatty…)? How is technical language made accessible?
3. **Literary devices**: Where are alliteration, parallel construction, humour, or pop-cultural allusions used, and to what end?
4. **Rhythm and structure** `[lite]`: How do changes in sentence and paragraph length affect readability and focus at key points?
5. **Voice and perspective**: How does the writer shift perspective, tense, or active and passive voice to match tone or write in character?

## 7. Art and multimedia

Skip this section when the article is text only.

1. **Visual contribution** `[lite]`: Do photos, charts, or maps add to understanding, or only decorate?
2. **Lead art and imagery**: How does the lead art or portraiture draw readers in and carry character or emotional themes?
3. **Data visualisation**: Does the data presentation clarify without misleading or dropping critical context?
4. **Placement and impact**: Are multimedia elements placed to enrich the text rather than distract from or repeat it?

## 8. Conclusion

Always asked, always last. Every answer must point back to something KB said earlier in the session.

1. **Key strengths** `[lite]`: What are the three most important things the writer did to make this story exceptional?
2. **Elevating quality**: Which three elements lift a good story into an excellent one?
3. **Close-reading observations**: What three observations from this reading do you want to remember for future work?
4. **Actionable improvements** `[lite]`: What four concrete steps will you take in your own reporting and writing because of this reading?
