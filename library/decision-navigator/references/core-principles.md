## 1. Protect survival before upside

**Principle:** Never take an uncapped risk that can impair the ability to continue operating merely because the expected upside looks attractive.

**If** a plausible adverse path threatens payroll, statutory obligations, critical liquidity, or the ability to keep operating, **then** reject the uncapped version and redesign it as a smaller, staged, insured, contractual, or otherwise bounded bet.

Questions:
- What is the maximum loss, not the expected loss?
- Can the business absorb the loss without emergency financing?
- Does the decision create a hidden working-capital requirement?
- Is the downside reversible before it becomes existential?

## 2. Define the real decision

**Principle:** A badly framed question produces a polished answer to the wrong problem.

**If** the user starts with an option ("Should I buy X?"), **then** restate the underlying decision ("What problem are we solving, and what are the alternatives including doing nothing?").

Always identify:
- desired outcome
- stake
- deadline
- default/do-nothing path
- constraints
- who owns the decision

This control catches framing, contrast effects, action bias, omission bias, and false either/or choices.

## 3. Take the outside view first

**Principle:** Before believing why this case is special, learn what happens in comparable cases.

**If** the decision depends on a forecast, **then** seek a reference class before building the detailed internal story.

Prefer several analogues over one memorable example. Include failures, near-misses, and ordinary outcomes rather than only successful cases.

Use the outside view to test:
- likely cost and schedule
- success/failure rates
- adoption and retention
- customer payment behavior
- hiring performance
- implementation effort
- downside frequency

Do not force a reference class when cases are genuinely incomparable. Explain the limitation.

## 4. Separate evidence from story

**Principle:** Narrative coherence is not evidence.

Classify important claims as:
- **Fact:** directly observed or reliably sourced.
- **Inference:** conclusion drawn from facts.
- **Forecast:** claim about the future.
- **Story:** explanation that may be coherent but is not independently established.

For causal claims ask:
- What is the proposed mechanism?
- What else could create the same pattern?
- Did cause precede effect?
- Is there evidence beyond correlation?

This control addresses story bias, false causality, halo, liking, availability, association, framing, contrast, and gibberish.

## 5. Try to kill the preferred answer

**Principle:** The first job after forming a thesis is to look for evidence that would make it wrong.

**If** the user or team has a preferred option, **then** state it explicitly and search for the strongest disconfirming evidence before adding more support.

Ask:
- What fact would make me reverse this decision?
- What would a skeptical outsider notice first?
- What evidence am I discounting because I dislike the implication?
- If the opposite option were proposed, what would I say?

Use a premortem for material decisions: assume the project failed and list the most plausible causes.

## 6. Think in ranges and alternative paths

**Principle:** A point estimate hides uncertainty and a single expected path hides risk.

**If** a decision has meaningful uncertainty, **then** use ranges and at least expected/adverse/favorable paths.

Where useful estimate:
- probability
- impact
- reversibility
- time to detect error
- time/cost to recover

Distinguish independent events from dependent processes. Do not invoke "it is due to turn" for independent events. Do not assume regression to the mean guarantees improvement.

For rare but severe events, probability and severity both matter. A 1% catastrophic risk is not "almost zero" simply because the percentage is small.

## 7. Preserve independent judgment

**Principle:** Discussion should combine judgments, not create them from the first speaker's anchor.

**If** several people will advise on a material decision, **then** collect their initial views independently before group discussion when practical.

Also:
- separate rank from expertise
- ask dissenters to state their case without social penalty
- do not treat silence as agreement
- ask each person what evidence would change their view
- assign one person to challenge the preferred option

This control addresses groupthink, social proof, authority bias, anchoring, and conformity.

## 8. Follow incentives and accountability

**Principle:** Understand what each actor is rewarded for, penalized for, and able to externalize.

For each important recommendation ask:
- Who benefits if this is approved?
- Who bears the downside?
- What metric is being optimized?
- Can the metric be gamed?
- Is the person accountable for the result or only the recommendation?
- Does a financial reward crowd out an existing non-financial motive?

Do not use unsupported claims such as "90% of behavior is incentives." Treat incentives as a major explanatory variable, not a complete theory of behavior.

## 9. Precommit the bet and the exit

**Principle:** Decide limits while calm, before sunk cost, ego, urgency, and hope accumulate.

Before starting a material initiative define:
- maximum committed loss/cost
- maximum time
- milestones
- review dates
- evidence that supports continuation
- evidence that invalidates the thesis
- exit or escalation conditions

Evaluate continuation using future incremental costs/benefits and opportunity cost, not past effort.

Owner heuristic: an actual loss beyond roughly 50% of the predefined bet should force a formal review and normally an exit unless strong new evidence improves the odds. This is a personal heuristic, not an empirical law. Exit earlier when the thesis is invalidated.

For auctions/negotiations, establish a value-based walk-away level before competitive arousal. Do not use a universal arbitrary percentage discount.

## 10. Separate process quality from outcome quality

**Principle:** Luck can make bad decisions look wise and good decisions look foolish.

Before action document:
- what was known
- what was assumed
- what probabilities/ranges were assigned
- why the decision was chosen
- what would change it

Afterward ask:
1. Was the decision process sound given what was knowable at the time?
2. What new evidence should change the rule for next time?

Do not rewrite the original reasoning after seeing the result.

## 11. Operating overlays

### Reversibility + liability

Use these two dimensions together. Low-cost but irreversible people/reputation/compliance decisions can deserve more scrutiny than larger but reversible purchases.

### Opportunity cost

A project does not need to be bad to deserve cancellation. A better opportunity can make it the wrong use of scarce attention or capital.

### Fixed deadlines

When the deadline is real:
1. preserve the core outcome
2. reduce scope
3. simplify the solution
4. spend more to accelerate only if the outcome still justifies it

### Mandatory change

For legal/compliance/safety/payroll obligations, do not create false optionality. Decide the implementation, transition, communication, and control design.
