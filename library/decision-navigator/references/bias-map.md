# Bias-to-Control Map

## How to use this file

Use this as an index, not a checklist. Identify only biases that plausibly affect the current decision, then apply the corresponding process control. The names below preserve the user's source-note vocabulary where useful, but several are heuristics, statistical errors, persuasion effects, or organizational failure modes rather than formal cognitive biases.

## Outside-view and statistical errors

- **Survivorship bias** → Build the reference class with failures, ordinary cases, and abandoned attempts, not only visible winners.
- **Swimmer body illusion / selection effect** → Ask whether observed traits caused the outcome or were selection criteria present before the intervention.
- **Clustering illusion** → Treat small-sample patterns as noise until tested; check expected random variation.
- **Regression to the mean** → Do not attribute ordinary reversion after an extreme observation to the latest intervention without a comparison.
- **Coincidence** → Estimate how many opportunities there were for the rare-looking event to occur; improbable events occur in large opportunity sets.
- **Base-rate neglect** → Start from relevant prevalence/success rates before case-specific details.
- **Gambler's fallacy** → Ask whether events are independent. Independent past outcomes do not create a balancing force.
- **Induction** → Past success raises evidence but does not make future success certain; ask what could change the generating process.
- **Beginner's luck** → Require repeated performance, comparison with peers, and sufficient sample before inferring skill.
- **Self-selection bias** → Check who chose to enter the sample and who is missing.
- **The average problem** → Inspect median, distribution, tails, concentration, and segmentation when skew matters.
- **Will Rogers phenomenon** → Check whether category changes mechanically improved both groups' averages without improving any underlying unit.
- **Exponential growth** → Model compounding explicitly and identify capacity/saturation constraints; do not extrapolate forever.

## Story, framing, and salience

- **Story bias** → Separate narrative from facts; ask what the story omits and who benefits from the framing.
- **Availability bias** → Replace memorable examples with frequencies/data where possible.
- **Contrast effect** → Evaluate absolute value and predefined criteria, not merely relative attractiveness to the comparison offered.
- **Halo effect** → Decompose the decision into independent attributes instead of extrapolating one salient trait.
- **Liking bias** → Evaluate the product/person against criteria as if the relationship were neutral.
- **Association bias** → Require a causal mechanism before using symbolic or repeated associations as evidence.
- **Framing** → Restate the same choice in gain, loss, absolute-value, and do-nothing terms.
- **Contagion bias** → Separate emotional association from functional value/risk.
- **Gibberish tendency** → Ask the speaker to restate the proposal in plain language, with mechanism, numbers, and falsifiable claims.
- **Because justification** → A stated reason is not automatically a good reason; evaluate its substance, especially for consequential requests.

## Belief protection and confidence

- **Confirmation bias (both forms)** → Write the belief first, then search for evidence that would falsify it.
- **Overconfidence bias** → Use ranges, base rates, calibration records, and adverse scenarios.
- **Hindsight bias** → Keep an ex-ante decision log and compare later beliefs with what was actually predicted.
- **Self-serving bias** → Separate controllable/internal causes from external causes; seek an independent attribution.
- **Cognitive dissonance** → Do not rewrite why the decision was made after new evidence; acknowledge the changed fact directly.
- **Illusion of invulnerability** → Reassess objective risk limits after success; success under excessive risk is not proof of safety.
- **Forecast illusion** → Track forecast accuracy and incentives; prefer ranges and scenarios to confident point claims.
- **Chauffeur knowledge** → Test depth with boundary questions, counterexamples, mechanisms, and admissions of uncertainty.
- **Authority bias** → Separate evidence/expertise from title, prestige, and confidence.

## Commitment, ownership, and escalation

- **Sunk cost fallacy** → Ignore past irrecoverable cost when deciding future incremental commitment.
- **Endowment effect** → Ask what price/effort you would accept to acquire the asset today if you did not already own it.
- **Effort justification / IKEA effect** → Evaluate output quality independently of effort already invested.
- **It will get better fallacy** → Replace hope with dated milestones and evidence thresholds.
- **Normalization of deviance** → Compare behavior to objective safety/risk limits, not to the absence of past incidents.
- **Scarcity error / reactance** → Ask whether scarcity changes fundamental value or merely creates urgency; preserve the option to walk away.
- **Winner's curse** → Set a value-based walk-away cap before bidding/negotiation and stop at it.
- **Loss aversion** → Reframe both action and inaction as gains/losses and compare expected outcomes; do not rely on a universal fixed multiplier.
- **Hyperbolic discounting** → Compare immediate rewards with discounted future value using a consistent horizon.

## Probability and alternative-path errors

- **Neglect of probability** → Evaluate probability and impact jointly; do not react only to vivid severity.
- **Conjunction fallacy** → A detailed subset cannot be more probable than the broader set containing it; strip away narrative detail.
- **Alternative paths** → Compare the observed path with other plausible paths that could have led to gain, loss, or ruin.
- **Outcome bias** → Judge the process separately from the realized result.
- **False causality** → Test temporal order, mechanism, confounders, and alternative causes before acting on correlation.
- **Fundamental attribution error** → Examine system, market, incentives, resources, timing, and constraints before blaming a person.

## Social and organizational effects

- **Social proof** → Popularity is evidence of adoption, not evidence of correctness; seek independent reasons.
- **Groupthink** → Collect independent views, protect dissent, do not treat silence as agreement, and challenge leader assumptions.
- **Belief in morality of the group** → Do not shift the burden of proof onto dissenters merely because the group sees itself as well-intentioned.
- **Social loafing** → Make ownership and individual contribution visible where possible; do not use fixed universal percentage losses.
- **Reciprocity** → Treat gifts/favors as separate from the transaction; ask whether the decision would change without the favor.
- **Motivation crowding** → Before adding money, ask whether the task currently draws on identity, duty, mastery, reciprocity, or intrinsic motive that a payment could change.
- **Incentive super-response tendency** → Map rewards, penalties, gaming opportunities, and externalized costs; incentives matter but do not explain all behavior.

## Action, control, and information

- **Illusion of control** → Split variables into controllable, influenceable, and uncontrollable; spend effort accordingly.
- **Action bias** → Include "wait/do nothing" as an explicit option when uncertainty is high.
- **Omission bias** → Treat harmful inaction as a decision with consequences, not as neutral absence of choice.
- **Information bias** → Define the specific unknown that can change the decision; stop gathering information once marginal decision value is low.
- **Paradox of choice** → Define criteria before comparing many options; shortlist rather than endlessly expand the option set.
- **Decision fatigue** → Reduce unnecessary decisions, use defaults/checklists for routine choices, and avoid claiming glucose is the established causal fix.

## Commercial persuasion effects

- **Anchor** → Generate an independent range or walk-away value before seeing an external number when possible.
- **Liking bias** → Separate seller from offer.
- **Reciprocity** → Separate favors from evaluation.
- **Scarcity** → Test whether deadline/availability changes fundamental value.
- **Contrast effect** → Compare with absolute criteria and alternatives, not the seller's reference price.
- **Framing** → Reframe in multiple equivalent ways.

## Personal/life effects with limited business use

- **Hedonic treadmill** → Relevant mainly to compensation, lifestyle, and personal goals; do not assume durable motivation from one-time material upgrades.

## Source-note claims not to encode literally

The following ideas may contain a useful direction but should not become precise operating rules without independent evidence:

- a fixed "3X" pain ratio for loss vs gain
- exact social-loafing percentages by team size
- "90%" of behavior explained by incentives
- a universal 20% winner's-curse haircut
- low blood sugar as the established explanation/cure for decision fatigue
- 0% and 1% risk being practically the same
- universal claims about gender and overconfidence
- "because" being sufficient justification regardless of content
- regression to the mean meaning a poor result will "soon" improve
